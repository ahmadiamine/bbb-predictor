Does this molecule reach the brain?

The blood–brain barrier is a sheet of cells packed so tightly that almost nothing slips between them. A drug has to dissolve straight through them — which means shedding the water clinging to its polar groups first. Draw a molecule and watch that logic play out.

How it works

  1. Measure eight things about the molecule

    Polar surface area, greasiness, hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, weight, flexibility, aromatic rings, and how three-dimensional the carbon skeleton is. RDKit computes all eight, here in your browser.

  2. Fit a logistic regression to 1,935 measured compounds

    Each one has a published measurement of whether it crosses. Nothing is simulated and nothing is inferred from a rule — these are laboratory observations.

  3. Test it on chemistry it has never seen

    Molecules are grouped by their core ring system, and whole groups are held out together. Families like the benzodiazepines never get split across training and test, so the model cannot answer by recognising a close relative.

  4. Read back what it learned

    Polar surface area dominates every other descriptor, with hydrogen bond donors second. That is the desolvation penalty, recovered from data by a model that was never told the barrier exists.

How well it does

Tested on molecules whose chemical family the model never saw during training — so it cannot score well by recognising a close relative it already memorised.

What this cannot do

  • Passive diffusion only. The barrier runs active pumps, notably P‑glycoprotein, which grab molecules that got in and throw them back out. Some compounds here look perfectly permeable and are ejected anyway. Eight descriptors cannot see a transporter.
  • Crossing is not the same as working. L‑DOPA reaches the brain by hitching a ride on an amino acid transporter, not by diffusing; dopamine itself cannot. That pair is the whole reason Parkinson's is treated with the precursor rather than the drug.
  • The labels are coarse. A yes/no flag compresses a continuous quantity — how much gets in, and how fast — into one bit, and different sources drew the line differently.
  • The classes are imbalanced. 77% of these molecules cross, because the dataset is built largely from drugs that were being developed for the brain in the first place.
  • Nothing here has touched a cell. A prediction is a reason to run the assay, not a substitute for it.
